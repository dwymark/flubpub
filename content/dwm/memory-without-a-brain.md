# Memory without a brain

Who are we but our memories and our dispositions? Memory is key to who we are. Naturally, this suggests that memory always *belongs to someone*. This is only a sliver of the truth. Memory is absolutely everywhere, once you know how to recognise it.

Michael Levin's planarian flatworm is the canonical example of a memory unlike our own. This flatworm is a strange little creature, equipped with a brain and a knack for regeneration. While a worm with a brain is already intriguing, it's really the tail where the interest is. Assuming you cut the head off first.

Cutting the planarian flatworm in half would be cruel. But if you do so for science, you see that being cut in half is not as bad for the worm as you might assume. The cells that make up the worm shimmer invisibly with a dynamic electrical pattern, its bioelectric field. The pattern is held remarkably steady. This is because the pattern is being defended against being disturbed by the cell's collective activity.

This bioelectric activity affects the way in which the worm's genes are expressed. It operates in the domain that the genome provides. In this case, the activity has a dominant role in the regeneration capacity of the worm. The cells in the amputated tail immediately get to work, dividing and specializing as necessary, until the tail has grown a head and the head has grown a tail; the bioelectric pattern contributes to this capacity.

The brain is nowhere to be found. Yet the solitary tail grows into a fully-formed, healthy worm as if it had never been injured in the first place. If you measure the bioelectric signal flowing through the worm while it is growing back its head, the pattern can be seen to settle towards its target. The electrical activity only comes to an equilibrium when the collective inhabits the form required by the pattern.

Levin's lab can reliably modify the stable biolelectric pattern on these worms by manipulating the ion channels cells use to maintain the bioelectric pattern. Cutting a population of worms with these modified patterns gives rise to a regenerated population where a quarter of the worms have two heads rather than a head and a tail. No gene was edited. And if you cut one of the double-headed worms in half again, it grows back as a pair of double-headed worms rather than two regular worms. This persists for the rest of the worm's lifetime. The pattern is an unfamiliar kind of memory, one that belongs not to an individual someone but instead to a collective someone.
