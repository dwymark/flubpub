# Memory without a brain

Who are we but our memories and our dispositions? Memory is key to who we are. Naturally, this suggests that memory always *belongs to someone*. This is only a sliver of the truth. Memory is absolutely everywhere, once you know how to recognise it.

Michael Levin's planarian flatworm is the canonical example of a memory unlike our own. This flatworm is a strange little creature, equipped with a brain, with a knack for regeneration. And while it is a bit odd to imagine a worm with a brain, it's really the tail where the interest is. Assuming you cut the head off first.

Cutting the planarian flatworm in half would be cruel. But if you do so for science, you see that being cut in half is not as bad for the worm as you might assume. The cells that make up the worm shimmer invisibly with a dynamic electrical pattern, its bioelectric field. The pattern is held steady with remarkable steadiness. This is no coincidence. The pattern is being defended.

The pattern encodes the flatworm's individual body plan. The fidelity of the representation is evidently quite high, considering the regenerative power of these worms. The cells in the amputated tail immediately get to work, dividing and specializing as necessary, until the tail has grown a head and the head has grown a tail; once the anatomy matches the defended pattern, it stabilizes.

Notice that the brain can be nowhere to be found. Yet the tail alone remembers what its missing half is supposed to look like! The memory is in the cells themselves, collectively, encoded in a voltage-bound signal that is actively maintained, using as much as a third of their total energy budget to do so. The pattern evolves dynamically and comes to rest stably only when the collective inhabits the form that the pattern requires. This gives them their surprising regenerative capabilities, and those capabilities can be said to have memory.

By manipulating the ion channels that are used by the cells to defend the bioelectric pattern, you can change the pattern itself. You can convince the worm to grow two heads rather than one. No gene was edited. And if you cut the worm in half again, you will not see the regular worm; instead, you will see another double-headed worm. This persists for the rest of its lifetime, independent of the nervous system. The pattern in the bioelectric signal acts as a kind of memory, maintained by the collective cells of the worm. This memory doesn't belong to a singular someone. It belongs to a group of someones.
